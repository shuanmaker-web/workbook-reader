@passage
The Countess of Chinchón, so the legend goes, exhibited all the classic symptoms. Chills and spells of fever took turns tormenting her. At times, the sweat poured off the noblewoman; at others, she could not keep a bite of anything down. Her husband, the 17th-century Spanish governor of Peru, concluded that she had [[1]] malaria.

In a desperate attempt to save her life, he reluctantly [[2]] and permitted her to drink a special mixture made with the bark of a local tree. This species was claimed [[3]] powerful medicinal properties and had long been greatly esteemed in indigenous cultures for its healing powers. Wonders never ceased, for the lady did recover. The life-saving tree was promptly named "cinchona," derived from her title.

[[4]] this tale is true to actual events is still disputed. However, what is undoubted is that the cinchona tree is indeed the source of the most reliable [[5]] mankind currently has for malaria. The cinchona tree is the official national tree of both Peru and Ecuador and is also known as the "quina." Without it, the malaria cure "quinine" wouldn't exist.
@end

@passage
The four o'clock family is not the bunch of neighbors you run into every afternoon at teatime. It is, instead, a group of nearly three hundred species of flowering plants.

These colorful plants are referred to as "four o'clocks," because their flowers [[6]] open late in the day. They then usually remain in this inviting pose until dawn, hoping to attract the attention of nighttime pollinators.

Pollinators, primarily insects, play a crucial role in plant reproduction by transferring powdery pollen from one flower to another. [[7]] bees commonly perform this role during daylight hours, four o'clocks are often fertilized at night by creatures like beetles, flies, crickets, and moths.

[[8]] Mirabilis jalapa, arguably the most high-profile among all four o'clocks, other helpful pollinators such as butterflies and hummingbirds can also play a reproductive role. Larger winged visitors are [[9]] nearer by the vibrant colors and sweet scent of the Mirabilis jalapa flowers themselves.

The main goal of these collaborative creatures is to utilize their lengthy tongues or beaks to acquire the nectar [[10]] is the sweet substance stored within every flower. Before advancing to the next Mirabilis jalapa plant, which may display shades of purple, pink, white, or red, they ensure they are adequately coated in pollen.
@end

@passage
With regard to how and when plants communicate with each other and their surrounding environment, human observers have only begun to explore the surface. This is the [[11]] shared by many researchers and experts in the field of plant communication. As we dig a little deeper into this [[12]], we are discovering more about how virtually all root-growing species "speak" their mind.

From towering trees to miniature mint, evidence suggests that all species use both electrical and chemical [[13]] to get their messages across. These mysterious methods allow leaves, for instance, to send electrical warnings to stems and roots about the presence of predators or pests. Once alerted, these collaborative plant parts can release toxins or strong scents to [[14]] unwelcome visitors. Similarly, roots can sense a lack of moisture in the soil. Then they fire off electrical pulses to instruct leaves to preserve as much [[15]] water as possible.

Other plants in physical contact with these communicating [[16]], or close enough to detect their electrical activity, can and do respond similarly. At present, we can't yet [[17]] with any certainty whether this is intentionally done to spread information beyond the individual plant. However, what we know for sure is that very clear messages are constantly [[18]] from leaf to leaf and branch to branch, sometimes crossing entire forests and fields in the process.

Even more incredibly, [[19]] of the animal kingdom are often included in these "discussions." These volatile chemicals released by fruit-bearing branches and vines, for example, can alert birds, beasts, and insects to the fact that their goods are ripe. Certain plants, like tomato plants and cactuses, are also able to produce ultrasonic [[20]]. These can be detected by insects, bats, and mice and are sometimes, believe it or not, even a call for help!
@end

@passage
Ever since the Bronze Age, humans have maintained a close relationship with the henna tree. A rich dye extracted from this precious plant was once used to color the hair of ancient Egyptian kings. The use and applications of henna then branched out significantly around five thousand years ago.

[[21]] From these places, it had later spread to many parts of the Middle East and South Asia. As is still the case today, leaves from the henna tree are widely used to dye leather, wool, cotton, and other materials in shades of orange or red. They are also harvested to create mixtures for dyeing hair and nails in a similar way. Eyebrows, beards, fingernails, and sometimes entire heads of hair are colored with this dye during religious ceremonies, festivals, and special occasions like weddings and birthdays.

[[22]] For this purpose, a paste is prepared by grinding henna leaves into powder and mixing this powder with a diluting agent such as water, tea, or lemon juice. It usually takes 48 hours for a special active chemical in the mixture to be released, after which it is applied directly to the skin. Various instruments, including twigs, feathers, pens, and brushes, are used for artistic application. In some regions, cake icing cones are employed, while Moroccan henna artists traditionally use syringes.

Henna stains or "tattoos" usually start off light red in color, growing gradually darker over time. [[23]] They then start to fade, vanishing completely within three weeks.

[[24]] As for minimum temperatures, these smooth-skinned, multi-branched, short trees rarely survive lows of five degrees and under.
@end
