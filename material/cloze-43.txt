It cost over a billion euros to construct and today generates enough electricity to power 800,000 Norwegian homes. Since commencing [[1]], it has more than doubled the country's capacity for producing wind power.

In total, the Fosen Vind project boasts 278 giant turbines. These stand over eighty meters tall, reaching into the crisp Scandinavian sky. They also have impressive wing spans wider than a football field. [[2]] these giant turbines rotate around the clock, they produce a combined capacity of 1.05 gigawatts (GW) of electricity, literally out of thin air.

The central Norwegian west coast, home to Fosen Vind's wind farms, [[3]] to be one of Europe's prime locations for generating this type of power. The region is extremely windy throughout the year, making it a(n) [[4]] for establishing wind farms. Among the half-dozen farms, two lie on the Fosen Peninsula while the Geitfjellet and Hitra sites are located on the nearby islands of Snillfjord and Hitra, respectively.

[[5]] this advanced wind power facility, creating clean, renewable energy in this part of the world is now a breeze.

@passage
Reason is the name of a very insightful short story written by acclaimed science fiction author Isaac Asimov. First [[6]] in 1941, it tells the story of two future humans working in outer space. Their task is to oversee the operations of highly intelligent robots on an important solar station. The purpose of this station is to collect "energy beams" from "the Sun" and then distribute this to the planets. The human inhabitants of these heavenly bodies can then enjoy [[7]] a dependable power source.

Now, nearly a hundred years after Reason first appeared, it seems that a new age in solar power is indeed [[8]]. Research into creating potential space-based solar stations is currently under way in China, the US, and Japan. [[9]] there are several significant benefits to harnessing the sun's radiation in this fashion, these three superpowers' interest in such a project makes perfect sense.

Without interference from Earth's shielding atmosphere or its clouds, far more solar energy could be collected and farmed in space. A station orbiting the planet at breakneck speed would also be able to avoid another [[10]] to such production. It's called "night," and it's the main cause of solar power harvesting being limited to daylight hours.

In light of all these considerations, there's good reason to pursue such futuristic energy possibilities.

@passage
Mankind goes to an awful lot of trouble to generate electricity, and we all know why. In the absence of this precious energy commodity, our appliance-assisted, well-lit lives would probably fall to pieces.

[[21]] The elephantnose fish is an example. This astonishing natural phenomenon not only has a long and thin, trunk-like mouth. It also has a built-in biological "battery" at the opposite end of its body!

This incredible internal organic power point is, not shockingly at all, known as an electric organ. [[22]] Though admittedly a mouthful, electrocytes are simply disc-shaped cells with a negative charge on the inside, and a positive charge on the outside.

Whenever the elephantnose fish is threatened by predators larger than itself, its brain sends very deliberate instructions to these cells. [[23]] In consequence, an electric current is generated which, assuming all the electrolytes are working together as is the norm, produces a single fishy volt (V). Though this is half a volt less than a manmade AA battery creates, it can nonetheless keep hungry visitors at least a meter away.

In addition to this, elephantnose fish also come equipped with unique skin cells which are tuned into the electric field surrounding them. [[24]] All in all, the elephantnose fish therefore generates and uses electric power and principles to great effect. What science fails to explain as yet is how exactly these curious creatures do not shock themselves silly in the process.
@end
