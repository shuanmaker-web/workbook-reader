@passage
The thought of traveling at speeds of over 200 kilometers an hour on a train might leave some of us a little dizzy. The notion of doing so without a human hand to steer operations might be even more unsettling for some. While fully driverless trains are technically feasible and operate on some routes worldwide, they still represent only a small fraction of the trains in use today. [[21]]

In Japan, extensive plans are on track. JR Tokai could launch just such a fully automated bullet train service by 2028. [[22]] This infrastructural development, they hope, will not only make these lines safer but also substantially reduce labor costs.

Of course, the smart sensors and other technology required to produce self-driving trains already exist. German rail operator Deutsche Bahn, in cooperation with local tech giant Siemens, has employed a completely driverless train in Hamburg for several years now. [[23]] It's thus giving railway users a taste of eco-friendly and more efficient future domestic train networks.

Beijing has also introduced an advanced driverless bullet train called the Fuxing. The train was designed to transport passengers at a remarkable speed, particularly serving Olympic venues. This cutting-edge train even boasts a complete onboard broadcast studio for journalists' needs. [[24]]

Should this type of AI-driven transportation indeed become standard engineering practice in coming decades, it has the potential to totally transform rail usage. One day, all train systems and metro networks could well be running like clockwork, completely unmanned.
@end
