@group 一、綜合測驗

@passage
We have the moon to thank for ocean swells and tides. These, in turn, bring about waves, which can be extremely powerful depending on winds and other factors. If they are powerful enough, we can even harness these natural forces to [[1]].

Producing wave energy requires what's called a turbine. This is a circular machine with large blades or other rotating parts that spin due to the continuous flow of air or water over them. As rotation occurs, this energy of motion can be [[2]] electricity.

Strategically placing turbines in areas where substantial and continuous ocean waves prevail [[3]] for sustained power generation as the waves keep the turbines in motion. Similar to how wind energy production relies on moving air, wave energy generation requires only sufficient volumes of moving water. Compared to conventional fossil fuels, it thus [[4]] a better renewable energy alternative.

Unfortunately, in spite of these advancements, current systems designed for harnessing the ocean's motion remain on a relatively small scale. [[5]], they can't yet be relied upon to power facilities or areas with large power needs. Since many variables apply when it comes to wave size and strength, it is also difficult to guarantee constant currents, either marine or electric.
@end

The global economy experienced a downturn due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite significant advancements in energy efficiency, [[6]] remains an increasing global demand for energy.

This is the view of America's Energy Information Administration (EIA). A federal agency based in Washington DC, the EIA has a(n) [[7]] for accurate energy-related reporting. In one of these recent [[8]], the EIA predicted that global energy usage will shoot up by almost 50% between 2020 and 2050. Development in budding Asian economies is forecast to be the main [[9]] behind this huge increase. Furthermore, traditional liquid energy in the form of oil and diesel will be fueling it.

Transportation will, in fact, be one of the sectors leading this steady climb in worldwide consumption. So too will industry, which means CO2 emissions are set to similarly increase in the next two or three decades. The Middle East will thus continue to play a major role [[10]] it will still be supplying most of the crude oil to keep such growth running.

In many parts of the world, therefore, energy seems to be stuck on an all-too-familiar fossil fuel track.

@passage
Engineer and inventor Laurence Kemball-Cook has spent a lot of time researching wind and solar power possibilities. However, when he finally came up with a(n) [[11]] way to generate eco-friendly energy, he started down a completely different renewable energy avenue. What Kemball-Cook's invention ran on wasn't particles of light or puffs of air, but [[12]].

Pavegen tiles are a special type of smart pavement block. Triangular in shape, these incredible little power cells can be laid along busy city walkways and pavements to generate electricity. Every time a pedestrian steps on a tile, it is gently [[13]] down, triggering a generator at each of its three corners. The more soles land on a tile, the more "manpower" can be [[14]] into electrical energy.

Currently, 270 Pavegen tiles covering a floor space of eighty square meters can, with the aid of just 120 walkers, [[15]] one kilowatt (kW) of power. This amount of electricity is enough to run a laptop for five-sixths of the day. It's also [[16]] to microwave 21 frozen pizzas, or dry twenty heads of wet hair with a hairdryer.

On a busy shopping street in the heart of cities like London, roughly 200,000 to 500,000 pedestrians [[17]] up and down all day and late into the night. In such regularly [[18]] pedestrian zones, these tiles have the potential to generate enough power to light up that entire road around the clock.

One final feather in Pavegen's cap is that its tiled systems can be used to [[19]] and record pedestrian numbers throughout metropolitan areas. Such data could help marketers to identify prime advertising [[20]]. It could also greatly assist with city planning and design.

Kemball-Cook's technology is still just taking its first steps in the clean energy industry. But there is no doubt that his sustainable solution has gotten off on the right foot!
@end

@passage
Few pastimes are as relaxing and refreshing as soaking in a steamy hot spring on a wintry day. Its pleasant, mind-clearing fumes gently energize fortunate bathers in a warm and comforting cloud. [[21]] Despite this, not many soaking souls give much thought to the geothermal forces at work that make such indulgence possible.

As scholars familiar with the word's Greek roots should be able to tell you, "geothermal" literally means "Earth's heat." The reason why we can enjoy hot springs is due to the thermal energy trapped in the crust of our remarkable planet. [[22]] Another is the energy left over from when the globe was formed billions of years ago.

Today, certain places scattered over its surface are what one might literally call geothermal hotspots. [[23]] Producing geothermal energy of this nature usually involves channeling underground hot water or steam sources in order to rotate turbines to create renewable energy which is far cheaper and more eco-friendly than fossil fuels, both in terms of extraction and usage.

[[24]] Over a quarter of the country's electricity needs are met with the aid of geothermal applications, and power production capacity in this field currently stands at just under 800MW. What's more, over 90% of the homes on this snowy but scenic island are heated in various geothermal ways. Best of all, this energy-giving earthly heat doesn't look like it will cool down anytime soon!
