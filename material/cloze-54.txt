@group 一、綜合測驗

@passage
It's a well-known fact that communities everywhere are often torn apart by two destructive "-ism" evils. One is racism; [[1]] is sexism. Both of these are superficial prejudices based on bias or stereotypes, and are often the result of cultural or social conditioning.

In short, they lead us to [[2]] judge others based merely on their gender or their skin color. Of course, we do so prior to actually attempting to get to know or understand those "others" first, and merely to seek solidarity with members of our own particular social group.

A third evil "-ism" that divides us further is ageism. This involves [[3]] preconceived notions about fellow community members, based purely on, as you might have guessed, how old they are. Assuming, [[4]], that all teenagers are poor decision-makers is a form of ageism. So too is taking it for granted that pensioners' views are outdated and worthless.

Like with most prejudices of this nature, the "cure" for this particular "-ism" starts with setting one's personal biases aside, and actively cultivating meaningful relationships with younger or older people. Following this, we can educate people of all ages on how to [[5]] and combat ageism.

Finally, we can elect leaders and policy makers committed to using the law to stamp out this age-old form of discrimination.
@end

Skimming through social media feeds, one can't help but feel this magnificent modern global community platform isn't really having the positive impact on society that it might. We have this incredible high-tech tool to connect [[6]]. And yet, we're wasting it on largely superficial discussions.

Most of the time, if we're honest, social media sites are accessed merely to comment on restaurant pics, or show off the latest holiday destination. It's also a common [[7]] for users to air casual opinions on politics or pop culture, without any desire to act on these views.

Luisa Neubauer, a young climate activist from Hamburg, is mobilizing social media to get everyone [[8]] more important things. During the recent COVID pandemic, people could no longer take to the streets to protest against climate change. Consequently, she made use of social media to [[9]].

She arranged to have people from all over post selfies with their signs and banners from home. She also organized mass email events and online calls to raise awareness. On one comment platform, Neubauer even inspired a "tweet storm" of carbon footprint comments, proving just how much support there was for socially [[10]] change.

Young people like Neubauer are showing just how society should be using this young technology.

@passage
Rarely do essays influence society for hundreds of years. Then again, Utopia, written by Sir Thomas More in 1516, is no [[11]] book.

Utopia tells the story of a judge and philosopher who meets a traveler to distant lands. It is from this [[12]] that he learns all about a fascinating and unique island situated in the New World.

The island is called Utopia, and there are various [[13]] of its society that set it apart from sixteenth-century Europe. For a start, the inhabitants of this island use no form of money or precious metals or jewels to [[14]] things. In fact, there is no private property at all. It is only children that amuse themselves with shiny little gems, and the gold used to weigh down their slaves, effectively "belongs" to each and every citizen.

Instead of shops and markets, all the goods produced on the island are [[15]] in community warehouses. Should anyone require something, they simply request it and it is handed out as needed. Resident Utopians have neither the desire to collect material [[16]] or wealth, nor the wish to display any riches or prosperity in front of others. Instead, each is taught agricultural skills and a basic trade such as metalwork or [[17]], so that they may contribute to the good of society.

Unemployment and salaries do not exist, and neither do long, taxing workdays. The islanders only [[18]] for six hours a day, and are urged to pursue a range of formative studies in their hours of leisure. Everyone enjoys the same [[19]], home-grown, home-cooked meals together in enormous dining halls and, should anyone fall ill, free hospitals welcome them with open arms.

As for [[20]], faiths of every description are equally tolerated and accepted, and priests are freely permitted to tie the knot. All citizens also share a common prayer that the right form of government will one day be revealed to them.
@end

@passage
It's no exaggeration to say that depression is one of the most common lifestyle illnesses in the developed world today. The huge market in developed countries for drugs to treat this serious mental condition is proof in itself. [[21]] Were it not for these treatments, in fact, many depression sufferers would not be able to remain fully functional members of their societies.

And yet, this is not to say that those living in the developing world simply do not get depressed. Far from it. Poverty, unemployment, natural disasters, and substance abuse can often have just as negative an impact on mental health as can the stress of living in a rat race in a developed country. [[22]] An estimated 85% of these poor souls, in fact, receive no treatment whatsoever.

StrongMinds is a charity organization with a mind to address this issue. Though based in New Jersey, its staff and volunteers currently also operate in two low-income, sub-Saharan African countries, Zambia and Uganda. [[23]] They also introduce other community- and school-based initiatives to increase awareness about depression, and how to cope with it.

So far, the non-profit has reached out to over three hundred thousand individuals. They're also happy to report that four fifths of these "clients" remain depression-free for at least six months after treatment.

[[24]] The more mental strength and peace of mind young people and mothers and aunts and sisters develop, the more able they are to be strong for others.
@end
