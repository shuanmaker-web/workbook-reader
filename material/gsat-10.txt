Have you ever felt that someone was standing a little too close to you? Maybe you even yearned to cautiously [[6]] or shove them away. If so, you're not alone.

We all have our own sense of personal space and it's quite [[7]] when someone intrudes on it. It's natural to feel anxious or uncomfortable. However, at its core, this sentiment may actually be less "personal" than cultural. For most Westerners, the ideal amount of personal space is around one meter. In Asia, however, personal space can be as little as a few centimeters. Since their countries are often more populated, Asians have become [[8]] to having much less personal space.

Touch also plays a part. While touching between acquaintances may be considered too [[9]] in the United States, in South America and southern Europe, physical contact is an essential part of conversation. This is an instinctive defense mechanism activated by the brain. As for individual [[13]], your personality is often significant. The more [[14]] you are, the more space you'll tend to uphold between yourself and others.

With all these variables to ponder, how can anybody be sure whether they're invading someone else's personal space? The easiest thing to do is to watch other people's body language. @passage
To many, reading seems like a tiresome pursuit. While books are commonly credited with broadening people's knowledge, some don't enjoy reading because it reminds them of schoolwork. However, reading doesn't have to be tedious. [[16]]

The expression "curl up with a good book" perfectly illustrates the refreshment that reading can provide. Studies have shown that it's the most effective means of achieving relaxation, outdoing more standard practices. In tested subjects, listening to music reduced stress levels by 61 percent, sipping a cup of tea by 54 percent, and taking a walk by 42 percent. Reading's effects are much greater, decreasing anxiety by 68 percent! [[17]]

Psychologists believe that the act of entering into a story is so distracting that it eases muscle tightness. So, if a fictional world is the calming factor, shouldn't TV accomplish the same results? It does, but not as well. People often turn to TV programs when they want to relax. The average American adult spends as much as 15 years of his or her life watching TV. [[18]] A story occupies the mind with information that diverts attention away from one's worries. In contrast, the minimal concentration needed by a television program still allows the mind to wander and focus on other things.

Reading produces an effect on the brain similar to that of meditations. According to researchers, when students read silently, they read four to seven words per second. Scientists determined decades ago that seeing four to seven flashes of light per second transports the brain into the Theta rhythm—the same phase in which meditation occurs. [[19]] Therefore, when the brain is reading, it is actually in an altered state of consciousness.

The next time you come home exhausted and reach for the remote, do your brain a favor and immerse yourself in a story instead.
@end

@group 四、閱讀測驗

@passage
Time compels us to live and interact with each other in a certain way. In fact, time is such an influential part of our lives that we may not realize that other people experience time differently. The truth is, how we use and perceive time is influenced by our culture. Therefore, examining how other cultures structure time can give us a more in-depth understanding of their customs and values, as well as our own.

One way to understand a culture's attitude toward time is to look at the culture's pace. You might have the notion that the people of a foreign culture are not productive because they walk slowly or take too long to accomplish things. This could be a sign that your own culture is fast-paced. If, on the other hand, the people of another culture appear rushed, perhaps your culture is simply slower-paced in comparison. In general, industrialized countries have the tendency to have faster rhythms of life. By contrast, in many South American and Arabian countries, people tend to live a more relaxed life.

A common English saying is "time is money," which means that every moment should be used productively. This attitude can be seen in the strict way in which North Americans structure time for appointments and meetings. Similarly, in Japan, most people live by the clock. This is reflected in the Japanese train system, which is known to always be on time. Nevertheless, not all cultures live under pressure from the ticking hands of the clock. For example, people in Mediterranean countries, such as Spain and Italy, have a more laid-back attitude. In these countries, people tend to eat lunch later, between about 2 p.m. and 4:30 p.m. After this, restaurants close for several hours, giving staff a chance to rest before returning to work, where they stay until very late.

In short, every country has a rhythm set by its people. Therefore, understanding a culture's idea of time can help you know when to hurry and when to take it easy. If you don't keep pace with the rhythm, you might just stumble and fall!
@end

@group 五、混合題

@passage
Coffee is the third most popular drink in the world after water and tea, and around 90 percent of North Americans say they regularly drink coffee. One reason coffee is so popular is that it has caffeine, which makes drinkers feel more awake and alert. Many people start their day by brewing a pot of coffee immediately after getting out of bed in the morning.

Unfortunately, coffee's popularity is causing devastating damage to the environment. Coffee was originally grown in the shade in between trees, which meant that it could grow naturally in forest areas without the need for dedicated farmland. In the 1970s, farmers began growing coffee in full sunlight because the plants would ripen faster and produce more coffee berries, helping them to sell more coffee. This meant the clearing of many forests to make space for coffee growing, and because over-farming eventually causes the quality of the soil to become poor, more land must be found every few years. Furthermore, employing the full-sunlight growing method requires lots of fertilizer, which causes problems when rain washes it into sources of drinking water.

Now many coffee farmers are returning to the shade-grown method to protect the environment, as well as their future livelihoods. The next time you reach for a cup of coffee, make sure it is genuine shade-grown coffee. It's not only good for the environment; coffee experts report that it tastes better, too.
@end
