@group 一、綜合測驗

@passage
Now and then, one comes across a tree or hedge or bush that looks for all the world like it's covered in spaghetti. [[1]] closer inspection, one finds that this decoration is not, in fact, pasta. It's witches' hair.

"Witches' hair" is a common nickname for Cuscuta or dodder, an intriguing plant found in both tropical and moderate climates. A "parasite" in nature, it [[2]] by living on, and feeding off, other plants. Just as a flea clings to the fur of some poor itchy domestic animal and dines on its blood, the parasitic dodder very rudely moves in and sponges off crops, trees, or flowering plants capable of producing their own nutrients. Since they themselves completely lack "chlorophyll"—that green coloring plants use to absorb sunlight—they are wholly dependent on these [[3]] hosts.

Once such accommodation has been found, the dodder wraps its thin vine-like stems around the nutrient-provider's leaves or branches. It then deploys a special kind of root, known as a "haustorium," to [[4]] this private property and access precious plant resources contained within.

[[5]], it is conceivable that certain plants less willing to be hosts have acid and organic compound defenses against such bothersome tenants. Some also cover themselves in fine, hair-like growths to prevent this "spaghetti" from attaching itself too firmly.
@end

@passage
Located at the focal point of Guyana's coat of arms, aptly named the "Land of Waters," is a remarkable depiction of a plant. At its core [[6]] a sizable, circular leaf, accompanied on each side by a captivating lotus-like flower. Notably, this particular water lily bears the unique distinction of being named after an English queen, recognized as Victoria amazonica.

As the Latin title suggests, the royal celebrity [[7]] was Queen Victoria (crowned 1837), and it is when surrounded by the Amazon Basin's slow-moving waters, that this lily feels most at home. In its natural environment, therefore, you're most likely to observe said flowers and giant leaf floating peacefully on a shiny Amazonian surface.

The leaf in particular is not hard to spot. [[8]] a diameter of over three meters, it is, in fact, the biggest leaf in the entire water plant kingdom. A massive green, fleshy dish lined with spikes underneath to discourage consumption, it can easily support the weight of a human child. Alligator-like reptiles known as "caimans" are often seen [[9]] on these leaves. Local lizards and birds too, stroll across them without any fear whatsoever of getting wet.

Next to these [[10]] fresh-water pads, even Victoria amazonica's splendid white flowers look rather small. Nonetheless themselves up to 40 centimeters in diameter and weighing over 1.5 kilograms, they only bloom for two or three days.
@end

@passage
If you've ever come across a soft, moist mat of green growing on the face of a rock, you've probably been introduced to "moss." Granted, such [[11]] are not particularly exciting. Without any roots or seeds to speak of, and without any internal tissues for conducting water and nutrients like most plants possess, mosses do not, [[12]], appear to offer many talking points.

The only time most hikers or waterfall admirers give these spongy land plants any thought at all, perhaps, is when a particular stepping stone or tree root is more slippery than most surfaces. Then this flat, flowerless plant pad is cursed [[13]] and the need for its very existence is called into question.

For those in the know, however, there's far more to moss than meets the [[14]] eye. Severely lacking in conventional plant parts they might be, but mosses more than make up for these [[15]] in other ways.

For starters, they quite often form the foundation for other plant life. Since they can retain several times their own weight in moisture, they create water-rich environments for other species to [[16]] in. At the same time, just their mere presence stabilizes the soil and prevents erosion, aiding plant [[17]] and forest formation even further.

In many ecosystems, moss also helps to [[18]] the temperature of the earth below. Themselves being able to withstand anything from nearly minus 270 to 100-degree-Celsius conditions, they can have either a cooling or warming effect, as is required. In the [[19]] of the environment, mosses are found in desert conditions photosynthesizing (using sunlight to convert CO2 and water into oxygen) at 40 degrees. They're also found growing six kilometers above sea level in some of the least [[20]] high-mountain settings on Earth.

Such staggering adaptive and life-nurturing merits are, in all likelihood, the reason why moss has been on this planet for over 400 million years.
@end

@passage
The ancient Far Eastern art of bonsai is one that's been passed on for dynasties. In short, it is the Japanese practice of growing a dwarfed tree specimen in a small, shallow tray of soil. Usually, moreover, this continues uninterrupted for very long periods of time.

Throughout these years, perhaps even centuries of devoted attention, its branches, trunk, and roots are consistently trimmed and trained. [[21]]

The result of all this constant expert care and diligent dedication is a spectacular, fully mature tree in miniature. [[22]] Depending on the species cultivated, they can even produce cones, berries, and fruit.

Considering all the time and effort needed to grow these living natural monuments, it's not surprising that the finest bonsai can fetch a high price. They're regularly sold at auction much like sculptures and paintings. [[23]]

One of the most highly valued bonsai trees in the world today is of the Japanese white pine variety and known simply as "the Yamaki pine." The Yamaki family had cultivated this woody work of art for no fewer than five generations at its Hiroshima nursery. It was then very generously donated to the U.S. National Arboretum, a federal botanical garden devoted to trees.

[[24]] It is over four hundred years old and is today valued at around $1.3 million. In select cases, it seems, money really does grow on trees.
@end
