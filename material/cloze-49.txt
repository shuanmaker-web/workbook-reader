This meadow [[1]] many small bushes made up of centimeter-thick, ribbon-like leaves that can grow up to a meter and a half long, with up to 17 parallel veins running through each leaf.

Together, these bushes form a vast colony that covers an area of seafloor about 5 miles wide and grows at a depth of 100 feet. This impressive meadow can absorb as much carbon as an area of rainforest of the same size can, [[2]] its extensive coverage and growth over 100,000 years.

Not only that, but this "Neptune grass," [[3]] named after the Greek god of the sea, has been purifying its native waters and providing a hospitable habitat for diverse communities of underwater plants and animals. For ages, the Neptune grass has been sustaining underwater ecosystems by sheltering them from currents and predators.

However, the increasing severity of marine pollution is leading to this ancient seagrass [[4]] and at risk of disappearing. To [[5]] this tragic tide, it's essential that we act quickly to reduce the amount of pollution entering the oceans. We cannot afford to delay conservation efforts to protect this "flowering fruit of the sea" that has flourished since prehistoric times.

@passage
If you happen to be keeping a houseplant for the first time, Sansevieria trifasciata is an excellent choice. It is extremely tolerant of low light conditions and irregular watering. What's more, studies have shown that this evergreen West African wonder can [[6]] and remove many of the harmful toxins found in indoor air.

The primary reason for the popularity of this plant, however, is its spectacular long and thin but stiff leaves. These sturdy leaves emerge from a circular leaf base near ground level, usually reaching up to a yard in length. They grow in clusters of three, [[7]] a deeper green hue as the plant matures, and come naturally decorated with eye-catching green and gray bands. On top of that, the leaves end in sharp tips at the top resembling flames. This striking feature often makes the plant a [[8]] choice for distinguished office corners and elegant doorways.

Due to these prominent [[9]] leaves, this popular pot plant is also often called Saint George's Sword. Its coloring and shape, as well as its African roots, have also earned it the alternative nickname of "snake plant." Finally, as a [[10]] to the sharpness of its leafy parts, Sansevieria trifasciata also goes by the humorous name of "mother-in-law's tongue." No matter what name it is known by, this plant stands out for its unique qualities and remains a sought-after addition to any indoor space.
@end

Not very far into the third decade of the 21st century, Texas lawmakers were compelled to take action. They were forced to declare two of the state's [[11]] plant species officially "endangered." These plants play an important role in preserving local biodiversity.

Rapid urban expansion in the state is gradually [[12]] habitats where wild flowers and other branched beings once flourished. In consequence, the existence of many of these plants is being threatened. Urgent federal [[13]], in fact, is necessary if they are to survive.

One of the species recently added to the endangered list is "prostrate milkweed." Indigenous to the [[14]], this rare member of the milkweed family nonetheless plays an indispensable role in the life cycle of monarch butterflies. The [[15]] prefer this natural nursery where they deposit their eggs in the spring, and the plant's steady decline is already impacting on their population. The more land is [[16]] up by human activity in the form of construction and drilling for oil, the more milkweed-sustaining soil is lost.

But for [[17]] governmental measures, a local purple wild flower would have continued to suffer the same fate. Several territories identified as critical habitats for the "bracted twistflower" are now protected by federal law. Digging up, cutting down, or even just picking this type of flower is no longer legal either. There are now hopes that the species will recover adequately in the next few years to once more thrive in the area.

Such a(n) [[18]] would, of course, be good news for local bees. These lively insects often call on wild flowers of this nature when [[19]] ingredients for their honey. This once again highlights just how important it is to keep a protective eye on all plant species. Compared to building a border wall or developing industry, a flower or flowering plant might seem tiny or entirely [[20]]. And yet, its vital role in Nature's intricate ways can never be underestimated.

@passage
Cactuses are not often the subject of fiction, yet these resilient desert plants can tell intriguing tales. With fleshy, thorny stems that store water, cactuses are well-suited to survive in the hot, arid conditions of regions such as the American Southwest.

One short story by renowned American author O. Henry features a cactus and its unfortunate owner. The owner was an arrogant man named Trysdale, who kept a cactus in his apartment. The desert plant was a gift from a special young lady he had hoped to marry. [[21]] However, in reality, he could hardly speak a word of this foreign tongue. When she complimented his supposed bilingual skills, Trysdale was too caught up in her flattery to tell the truth. He was acutely aware that her fondness for him was in large part based on white lies and vain boasts. [[22]]

Instead of giving an immediate "yes," the lady shyly promised to send her answer the following day and Trysdale generously agreed, confident she'd shortly be his wife. The next morning, when the young lady's servant arrived, all that awaited Trysdale was an odd-looking cactus labeled with an even odder scientific name: "Ventomarme." [[23]] Even when he saw her again days later, his hurt pride refused to beg for an explanation. She, for her part, turned just as cold at his emotionless response. As a result, their once intimate relationship was left in tatters.

[[24]] The cactus signified the woman's implicit message that she accepted his proposal—it was also a final test to see if Trysdale would be honest with her.
@end
